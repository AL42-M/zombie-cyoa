# THE HUSH — storybible

This is the canon for THE HUSH. Read it before writing or editing any creative text: story passages,
choices, hints, pop-ups, keepsakes, endings, and UI copy. Record the user's creative decisions here.

> **IMPORTANT, for every agent.** If the user proposes a change that contradicts something

## 1. Premise

- On **April 11**, every radio in the world went silent for **nine seconds**: the Hush. People went
  out into the street to look at the sky. Then a low, steady **hum** began, on the air and out of
  the mouths of the people it had taken. It has not stopped.
- The story is a **found survival journal**, written by an unnamed, ungendered narrator. It begins
  on **day 41**, in Rosco Drug & Sundry on Ferry Street, in an unnamed American town in an unnamed
  county.

## 2. What the hum is

### The truth (never stated outright)
- It is not a disease. It is a **signal** that recruits people.
- Once someone is fully taken, the note sustains their body. They need no breath, food, or water.
- **There is no way back.** A turned person is a host: something that spreads the hum, and nothing
  more.
- **Some habits carry over**, such as Holloway tying a sailor's knot in his empty hand, or the blind
  bass singer's note. Observers and hospital staff take these as signs that the person is still in
  there. They're wrong, but the story never says so outright.
- **"It Was Never An Infection"** lets the narrator stay aware after turning. That's a storytelling
  device so the ending has something to say, not a fact about the turned.
- **The wider world (background only):** humanity doesn't die out, but it slides back into
  scavenging and hunting, with the turned as a new predator. This is beyond the scope of this story.
- The name "infection" is the survivors' mistake. The game uses it because the characters do. The
  Blood status measures how far the note has taken hold.

### The source
- The signal can be heard on radio, at **1476 kHz**, transmitted with power when nothing else has
  any.
- **Who runs it, where it comes from, and why must stay a mystery. Permanently.**
  - No hints of a military base, a research station, or a mast on a hill.
  - No answer to aliens, the military, an invasion, or something supernatural.
  - The player decides.
- The **pulse** under the signal stays constant. It does not speed up or change.

### Misdirection toward the sky
- When idle, the turned look up at the sky, which makes readers and characters suspect aliens.
- That's intentional. People reasonably wonder about aliens. The story never confirms it.
- The real tell is radio: the signal arrives from above, through open space, where radio travels
  most freely.

## 3. The turned: behavior rules

### Idle state
- When not pursuing anyone, the turned **turn their faces up toward the sky**, mouths hanging open,
  humming. This holds whatever the weather: rain, snow, cloud.
- The hum is low, guttural, and choral, like a male choir holding one chord. Every voice is exactly
  in tune with every other.

### Needing sky
- **Indoors** they look for a window, a door, a skylight, a vent: any line of sight to the sky.
- **A glimpse is enough.** They don't need to be directly under open sky. They can stand at an angle
  under a culvert mouth, or under a roof vent.
- **With no sky at all**, they fumble around restlessly, like ordinary zombies.

### Spreading out
- In open areas they **spread out evenly, then stand still**, as if each of them were an access
  point carrying the hum into every space it can reach. The characters notice this in Act 3.
- **When one stops humming,** for any reason (killed, crippled, or for no visible reason), the
  local group shifts until the gaps between them are equal again, as if correcting the spread of
  the hum. It is quiet and unsettling, never explained.
- **"It Was Never An Infection" (end_hush):** the line that they stand where they are needed fits
  this rule. Where they stand facing walls or in enclosed spaces, there must still be some glimpse of
  sky.

### Senses
- **The turned never blink.** That's the first thing a careful observer notices, and Odile is the
  one to spot it. Without blinking, the eyes dry out:
  - **Recently turned:** bloodshot, raw, crusted at the rims, pupils still visible.
  - **Long turned:** the surface scars over to an opaque chalk white, with no pupil.
- Survivors can read the eyes as a rough clock of how long someone has been turned.
- **The culvert woman (Act 2)** is a recent one, with red eyes. The group notices and wonders why.
  If Odile is present and on reasonable terms, she offers the explanation. Later in the game, two or
  three choices let the narrator comment on a turned's eyes and what they mean.
- **They don't see; they listen.** Their eyes go unused.
- Continuous sound draws them more than loud sound: "A scream turns heads. A man coughing all night
  turns a street."
- When they are drawn to a sound, they leave their idle pose and move toward it. They are fast when
  provoked.

## 4. How a person is taken

### The assault
- **They are violent, but their violence has one aim.** The turned don't tear people apart, bite
  out throats, or eat anyone. They want the jaw open and the note going into the mouth. In pursuit
  of that they are relentless and brutal, the way an assailant is: the violation is the point.
  - They restrain the victim and take hold of the jaw to pry it open.
  - They often work together, one pinning the arms while another works the jaw.
  - All of them hum in unison throughout.
  - They don't punch or kick for the sake of it. The harm comes from grip and force.
- **They do not let go.** If someone tries to pull them off, they grip harder and use every bit of
  force their bodies can manage to keep their hold and keep humming into the mouth. They will:
  - break the jaw itself
  - break the victim's bones
  - crush a windpipe when they are holding a throat and the victim is dragged away
- **Pulling them off or reasoning with them is useless.** Survivors learn that the only way to end a
  grab is to hurt the turned badly enough that they can't continue, or to kill them. The turned are
  still alive by the rules of this story, so killing them is a real act, with real weight.
  - **Treat them as hosts.** Whatever remains of the person has no say in what the body does.
- **It ends in one of two ways:**
  - The victim is freed by force.
  - The victim starts humming **in agreement**. They are released at once, and every attacker goes
    straight back to idle: emotionless, faces tipped up, mouths open, humming.
- **Injuries are common.** Examples:
  - a strip of skin torn from a forearm by a grip that wouldn't give
  - fingers dislocated, a jaw cracked
  - Bram's eye on Ferry Street: a thumb pressed into the socket while one of them tried to grip his
    face, nearly gouging the eye out
- **Hospital.** In St. Amaranth Mercy the turned are kept either injured past the point of acting,
  or strapped securely to beds and chairs. Unstrapping them is dangerous.
  - **Mouth care.** Ward 11's nurse swabs the turned patients' mouths because the endless humming
    dries and cracks their lips and throats until they bleed or choke.
  - **How the turned die.** Hosts outside die the same way, now and then; the nearby group then
    shifts to even out the gaps. Don't over-explain why they don't all die of it.
  - **Say "mouth care" sparingly.** Explain it once, in the scene with that nurse; otherwise describe
    the swabbing.
- **Their intelligence is the real horror.** The turned seem as dumb as bricks: they hum, chase, and
  try to sing into you. But they are smart enough to recognize something blocking their way in and
  remove it the way a person would.
  - **The key scene:** a host peels the duct tape off Teddy's mouth so it can hum into him. The
    violence isn't what's frightening; it's the hint of human-level intelligence.
  - **Teddy is freed within seconds** and is not infected. The bible's "Teddy never infected" still
    holds.
- A bite or a wound **does not** spread the hum.

### What actually takes hold (raises Blood)
- **The note gets in only through the mouth and ears.** It takes one or more turned humming right
  at your mouth or into one or both of your ears.
- **Strongest:** being held with the jaw open while they hum into you.
- **Willing:** humming along yourself.
- **Weak:** prolonged or dense exposure, such as standing among a packed crowd of them.
- **Ordinary listening does not turn anyone.** Every living character hears the hum all day.

### Turning by choice
- Turning willingly takes more than listening. You have to **hum in agreement**.
- **Surrender counts as agreement.** "Stop holding the note down" means ceasing to resist the
  constant pressure of the hum. That is enough, and it doesn't need to be spelled out. The hum is an
  assault on free will, so stopping resisting is the same as accepting it.
- **Clean narrators can still join.** Anyone can choose this, even with no prior exposure; that is
  the twist of "It Was Never An Infection."

### The myth of bites
- **Ordinary survivors believe bites spread it,** because everyone who turned was bitten during the
  struggle. That's why the Marlowe House sign says "IF YOU ARE BITTEN" and the Marlowe House woman
  talks about bites.
- **Hospital staff know better.** They say "exposed", "held" or "grabbed".
- The narrator may notice the difference, but **nobody explains it.**

### The four-day clock and the cure
- The gray serum is drawn from turned patients' blood (the "yield"). It stops the change only before
  the note "reaches the bone."
- **In the game:** the serum fails if **more than four days** have passed since the first real
  exposure, or if Blood is already deep (infection 34 or higher). The second case covers heavy
  exposure, such as being held for a long time. This applies to every dose, including vials the
  narrator carries away.

## 5. Characters

- **Teddy (Teodor Alafuzo).**
  - **Who he is:** seventy-one, a choirmaster for about forty years. He lost a lung years ago, and
    the cold stockroom has given him **pneumonia**. **He is not infected, and never was.**
  - **Why the turned come for him:** his loud, continuous breathing draws them. That is the only
    reason.
  - **Deliberate misdirection:** readers raised on zombie fiction will assume he's turning, and may
    abandon or kill him. When they learn the truth later, they should feel they let an innocent man
    die, or killed one.
  - **Make it weird:** Teddy hears the turned as a musician would. He comments on their note, on how
    perfectly every voice is in tune, and on how much rehearsal a choir needs to manage that, when
    these people do it without effort.
  - **Only Teddy talks about music in technical terms** (note names, bars, pitch). Everyone else
    describes the hum in plain, everyday words.
  - **Odile's diagnosis is hedged.** She says "probably" or "likely" pneumonia, never certain. In the
    same scene, someone doubts her and is afraid that keeping Teddy puts them all in danger.
- **Odile Marchetti:** a veterinarian with thirty years in practice. Clinical. She never uses
  contractions.
- **Bram Kessler:** a corrections officer for eleven years. Procedural and measured. His daughter is
  nine, asthmatic, and lives in Thornbury with her grandmother.
- **Wren:** fourteen. She hasn't made a sound since the third day, and writes on a pharmacy pad in
  lowercase, without apostrophes. Her notes use their own visual style (class `pad`).
- **The Matron:** head of nursing at St. Amaranth Mercy; "Matron" is the staff's old-fashioned title
  for her. Precise and formal. She keeps the ledger.
- **Dr. Aytoun:** tired and plain-spoken.

## 6. Key story facts and images

- **The truck trailer on Quay Road, sealed since April 11.**
  - **The scene:** about sixty turned stand inside. A **roof vent** lets in a line of sky; they
    stand beneath it with their faces raised. A child's shoe lies in the cleared space in the middle.
  - **What the shoe is:** a coincidence. Characters who see it read meaning into it because they
    don't yet understand the sky connection.
  - **Later:** a narrator who actually saw the shoe may question what the turned were really doing,
    because the shoe doesn't make sense. Nobody who didn't see it may refer to it.
- **The signal as explanation.** It must be reached on exactly one path per run, never both:
  - **(i)** The Matron's records show the very first admissions, on April 11 and 12, were people
    listening to radios or headphones when the Hush struck.
  - **(ii)** Hospital staff have noticed that patients go quiet when the radio is switched off, and
    get restless when it is on.
- **The aliens question.** On a run that saw the telescope at Marlowe House:
  - **Bram** half-jokingly wonders whether its owner saw something come down.
  - **Odile** dismisses it.
  - **Wren** writes "or someone wanted us looking up".
  - Keep it small and never confirmed.
- **Ward 11.** Patients strapped in beds by the windows lie calm and humming. Those in the inner beds
  strain against their straps toward the light. This is why the note on Holloway's bed asks for his
  window to be opened.
- **Marlowe House sign of life.** By night it's a moving candle on the ninth floor. By day it's the
  glint of an amateur astronomer's telescope, and the narrator works out what it must be.

## 7. Style and naming

- **Language and voice:**
  - American English, and the setting is the US.
  - The game must feel *weird*, not generic zombie fiction.
  - Repetition kills a story. No choice may repeat an action the reader has already been offered
    in the same run. If a choice really must come back later in a run, its label AND hint must be
    rewritten so that no button ever looks reused, even word for word.
    - **Landmark names so far:** the Arimathea Chapel, Rockton's Field, Old Cleveholt Road, the Sallow
      Walk.

    - **Words describing the sky-gazing:**
      - Vary them, and don't overuse "sky". Use "faces tipped upward", "chins raised", "staring up
        through the vent", "mouths open to the rain".
      - In end_hush, "facing walls" becomes "tipped to whatever opening they can find".
      - Check how often "sky" appears in a single run and keep it low.
- **Rosco's.** Rosco Drug & Sundry is a local, family-owned drugstore that sold medicine, not a
    chain like CVS. Generic references say "the drugstore", never just "the store".
- **Place names.** Only landmarks need proper names: a chapel, a field, a road, a factory. Rooms
    inside a named building, and small unremarkable structures such as the culvert, stay as they are.
- **Blood values follow §4.**
    - Big jumps come only from being held with the jaw open, or from humming in agreement.
    - Dense, close exposure, such as walking inside the packed trailer crowd, counts as heavy even if
      brief.
    - Merely being near the turned, or hearing them on a ward, adds nothing or almost nothing.
- **Bram's eye** (Ferry Street route only).
    - In the struggle, a turned man's thumb nearly gouged his eye from its socket.
    - On those runs only, his poor sight shows in later text.
    - At least one later choice in Act 2, and one in Act 3 if he is still alive and not turned, must
      reflect it.
- **The blind man.** He appears on **Quay Road**, at the start of Act 2. He was blind before the Hush:
    his eyes were damaged and removed after an electrical fire at a local factory. Even so, he stands
    with his face tipped upward and humming, like the rest.
    - **Who recognizes him:**
      - **Teddy, if present.** The man sang bass in his choir, and Teddy remarks, almost proudly and
        horribly, that he's "finally hitting his note".
      - **Without Teddy, the narrator** recognizes him.
        - If Bram is on good terms with the narrator, the narrator tells the group.
        - If the narrator has pushed Bram away (told him to leave, or his trust is very low), the
          narrator keeps it to themselves.
    - **How to tell it:** write it as ordinary story text, with no explanation. The reader draws the
      conclusion.
- **Bram never leaves in Acts 1–2.** Even if told "Go to Thornbury, then. Alone." he stays, and the
    text should acknowledge that he stayed.
- **The narrator's gender stays ambiguous.** Never gender the narrator in the text. The user may call
    the narrator "he" in conversation; that is not a canon decision.
- **Names:**
  - Locations need plausible, specific names rather than generic labels such as "the church" or
    "the school". Rosco Drug & Sundry keeps its name.
  - Keep distinctive proper names, rather than replacing them with generic American ones.
  - The user's chosen exceptions: betting shop, the map of Cornwall, lime trees on the hospital
    avenue (the pits use "quicklime").
